# Design decisions

The decisions below are selected from the dated development record, rewritten for this case study. They explain intent and trade-offs; they are not a public copy of the private decision register.

## 1. Let an answer continue to count

**Problem:** a natural-language brief loses value when the next screen asks the customer to fill in the same information again.

**Options and reasoning:** the 10 November review rejected simply hiding the step numbers of the existing wizard. The underlying state and question selection had to change.

**Chosen UX:** one conversation with a persistent draft and contextual tools, rather than a general form after the assistant’s reply. **Implemented / Demo.**

**Intended benefit:** less repetition and a clearer relationship between what the customer says and what the interface asks next. It still needs broader usability validation.

## 2. Clarify the boundary, not every field

**Problem:** “clean the fridge” can leave a meaningful scope question; a request that already specifies a method should not trigger the same choice again.

**Reasoning:** a documented default is appropriate only when its scope is clear. Missing or contradictory information calls for a focused question; an unsupported request should remain visible without becoming a promised service.

**Chosen UX:** named contextual choices, explicit notes and human handling for exceptions. **Implemented / Demo**, with selected interpretation cases **Tested**. This describes the intended behavior, not a guarantee that every AI response follows it.

## 3. Keep interpretation separate from authority

**Problem:** a fluent answer can make an invented service, price or availability seem credible.

**Options and reasoning:** allowing the language model to determine a commercial result was rejected in favor of validated operations and a deterministic domain engine.

**Chosen UX:** the assistant helps structure the request; the application calculates and displays the quote and checks eligibility. **Implemented / Demo.**

**Intended benefit:** a result that can be inspected and recalculated independently of the wording generated by AI. Synthetic catalog rules still require commercial and safety validation before real use.

## 4. Make time a scope decision

**Problem:** reducing the number of hours without explaining the task consequences creates an unclear promise.

**Reasoning:** the project models complete tasks and constraints rather than a proportional “clean everything faster” discount.

**Chosen UX:** an interactive duration and mission summary exposes task selection and the resulting price. Customers can prioritize the work; some choices may be infeasible. **Implemented / Demo**, with historical checks. The screenshot in the README shows an earlier version of this control.

**Intended benefit:** let the customer understand a trade-off before approving it. No measured conversion or satisfaction improvement is claimed.

## 5. Ask about relevant equipment

**Problem:** knowing the task is insufficient when responsibility for equipment is unclear.

**Reasoning:** a long repeated checklist adds friction, while a single generic confirmation can hide special requirements.

**Chosen UX:** distinguish the supplier of equipment from the person confirming readiness, and emphasize requirements related to the mission. The cleaner’s brief uses the same operational context as the customer’s mission. **Implemented / Demo.**

**Boundary:** declarations do not certify equipment, chemical compatibility or actual availability. Material exceptions may still require human review.

## 6. Preserve continuity without pretending memory is complete

**Problem:** a familiar property or a replacement cleaner should not force every relevant detail to be rediscovered.

**Reasoning:** saved conversations, approved mission records and consented long-term preferences are different things. Reusing a one-off instruction as a permanent preference would create another kind of ambiguity.

**Chosen UX:** conversation history and approved briefs are **Implemented**; persistent property memory and complete versioned handovers are **Designed**. A new cleaner is intended to check the relevant requirements independently.

## 7. Keep the human exception visible

**Problem:** an incident can be mistaken for a service failure attributable to one person when the cause may be access, equipment or transport.

**Chosen UX:** record the context and let management document a resolution. **Implemented / Demo.** Full recovery and aftercare workflows are **Designed / Simulated**.

The prototype also includes automated response-window behavior. Its fairness and operational consequences require review before any real deployment; it is not presented as a validated employment or quality policy.

## Selected evolution

| Date | Change in direction | What it taught the project |
|---|---|---|
| 9 November | Server-backed application chosen over the earlier local simulation proposal. | Shared state and authorization are part of the product, not deployment details added at the end. |
| 10 November | A form-like wizard was replaced by continuous conversation and contextual tools. | Conversational copy alone does not create a conversational workflow. |
| 11 November | Scope, equipment choices and item-specific corrections were refined. | Small ambiguities can propagate into tasks and estimates. |
| 12 November | Provider failures and semantic verification failures were investigated separately. | Diagnostic evidence is more useful than guessing from an error message. |
